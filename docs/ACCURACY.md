# How the numbers are computed

Every figure this app shows is derived, and several common derivations are
wrong in ways that look plausible. This documents what each number means, how
it is produced, and how far to trust it.

All the logic described here lives in `src/lib/geo.ts` and is exercised by
`npm run verify`.

---

## Distance

Haversine on a sphere of radius **6 371 008.8 m** (the WGS-84 mean radius),
summed segment by segment over every point.

**Known simplification: distance is horizontal only.** The elevation component
is not added, so a track reads slightly short. The error is proportional to
`1/cos(slope)` — about 0.5% at a sustained 10% gradient, and under 0.1% for
most mixed routes. It is not worth the noise it would import from the DEM: on a
densely recorded track, adding a per-segment vertical component multiplies
elevation jitter into the distance figure, which does more harm than the
correction does good.

## Elevation

### Why raw gain is wrong

The obvious implementation — sum every positive difference between consecutive
points — is what most simple tools do, and it over-reports badly. A GPS or
barometric altimeter jitters by a metre or two at rest, and a track recorded at
1 Hz has thousands of samples for that jitter to accumulate across.

The verify harness reports both figures for every file in `gpx/`. The gap is
largest exactly where you'd expect — on tracks actually recorded by a device,
rather than generated by a planner:

| Track | Source | Raw gain | Filtered | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 95 km, 1557 pts | Wikiloc recording | 1086 m | 986 m | −9.2% |
| 64 km, 5308 pts | Wikiloc recording | 1950 m | 1778 m | −8.8% |
| 71 km, 3241 pts | OsmAnd recording, 1 Hz | 1526 m | 1261 m | −17.4% |
| 163 km, 3598 pts | planner output | 899 m | 874 m | −2.8% |
| 34 km, 412 pts | planner output | 283 m | 283 m | 0% |

Planner-generated tracks barely move, because their elevation is already smooth.
That pattern is the point: the correction is eating measurement noise, not real
climbing.

### What the app does instead

**1. Smooth over distance, not over samples.** Elevation is passed through a
moving average with a **60 m window measured along the path**
(`ELEVATION_SMOOTHING_WINDOW_M`). A fixed sample-count window would behave
differently on a 1 Hz recording than on a 1-per-10 m planner export; a distance
window gives the same answer for the same terrain.

**2. Accumulate with a noise floor and direction hysteresis.** Gain and loss use
a **3 m floor** (`ELEVATION_NOISE_FLOOR_M`), but only *reversals* have to clear
it. Once a direction is established, further movement the same way counts
immediately.

The hysteresis matters. A plain threshold — "ignore any change under 3 m" —
silently discards whatever part of a climb sits below the floor at the end. The
verify harness catches this directly: a synthetic 100 m climb in 1 m steps
reports **99 m** under a plain threshold and **100 m** with hysteresis. Meanwhile
a dead-flat 400-sample track jittering ±1 m reports **200 m** of gain unfiltered
and **0 m** filtered.

### Min and max altitude

Taken from the smoothed series, so a single spurious spike does not set the
maximum. Smoothing over 60 m moves a genuine summit reading by a negligible
amount.

## Slope

**Always measured over a real distance denominator.** Taking the gradient
between two adjacent points of a dense recording divides a rise of a few
centimetres by a run of a couple of metres, producing percentages that swing
wildly and mean nothing.

- **Track colouring** measures gradient over a **40 m** look-back window
  (`SLOPE_WINDOW_KM` in `src/components/ColoredTrack.tsx`).
- **Profile bars** measure it between consecutive bars, which are themselves
  spaced by distance (below), so the denominator is always meaningful.

---

## The elevation profile

Bars are sampled **by distance, not by array index**: the track is walked
accumulating real path length, and a new bar is emitted only once at least
`total / 200` km has passed (floor 20 m). Index sampling would put many bars in
the slow, densely recorded sections and few in the fast ones, distorting the
horizontal axis.

Maximum 200 bars regardless of track length. The map polyline is *not*
decimated — it always draws full-resolution geometry.

## Elevation sources

Elevation comes either from the file's own `<ele>` tags or from one of three
sources, in order of what the binary is told to use:

| Source | Resolution | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-hosted opentopodata (`-elevation-host`) | your dataset | Wins when set — an explicit choice |
| Terrain tiles (default) | ~30 m | Terrarium rasters read locally; caches to disk |
| Open-Meteo (`-elevation-tiles=false`) | 90 m | Copernicus GLO-90, public, daily quota |

- **90 m postings smooth out exactly the short, steep pitches that matter on a
  trail.** This is not hypothetical. Sampling one point in the Pyrenees
  (42.65, −0.04) gives **1920 m** from the 30 m tiles and **1539 m** from
  Copernicus 90 m — a 380 m disagreement, because a 90 m cell averages across
  ground that moves that much. Two points on flat terrain nearby agree to
  within 2 m. Prefer a 30 m source for anything mountainous.
- A DEM samples the **ground surface**. It does not know about bridges,
  tunnels, or embankments, and in forested terrain SRTM sits somewhere in the
  canopy rather than on the track.

**Reading a raster is itself interpolation.** The tile provider samples
bilinearly between the four surrounding posts, because nearest-neighbour would
step the profile in ~14 m terraces that the slope maths would then read as
walls. Every DEM service does the same internally, but it is worth stating
plainly: between posts, the surface is a straight-line assumption, and no DEM
knows what the ground does in the 30 m between two measurements.

**Measured vs interpolated.** Planned routes fetch a real DEM reading for every
point. Only past **6000 points** (`MAX_ELEVATION_LOOKUPS`) does the app query an
evenly spaced subset and interpolate between them — and when it does, it says
so, both in the route panel and above the elevation profile.

This matters because it used to lie. An earlier version sampled every 20th point
of the routed geometry, linearly interpolated the rest, and wrote the
interpolated values into the saved GPX as if they had been measured. On OSRM
geometry, 20 points can span several hundred metres, so any hill shorter than
that was erased. Interpolated elevation is now always labelled and never
silently persisted as fact.

## Time and speed

Only available when the file carries `<time>` on its track points — recordings
have it, planned routes do not.

- **Moving time** counts only segments where speed is at least **1 km/h**.
- Segments implying over **400 km/h** are discarded as GPS glitches and do not
  contribute to the maximum.
- **Average moving speed** is moving distance ÷ moving time, which is the figure
  worth comparing between rides. Overall average including stops is also
  computed but not currently displayed.

---

## Longest fuel gap

Shown only once the fuel POI layer is loaded. Every track point within **3 km**
of a fuel station is treated as a refuelling opportunity; the figure is the
longest run of track between two such opportunities.

Caveats worth keeping in mind:

- It is **along-track distance to a nearby station**, not routed distance to
  reach it. A station 3 km away across a ravine counts as reachable.
- It depends entirely on OpenStreetMap coverage. A rural station that nobody has
  mapped does not exist as far as this figure is concerned, so treat it as a
  **pessimistic** bound — and never as a guarantee that a mapped station has
  fuel, is open, or takes your card.

## Simplification

Ramer-Douglas-Peucker, run iteratively (an explicit stack, not recursion, so a
20 000-point recording cannot overflow). Perpendicular distances are computed in
metres via a local equirectangular projection, accurate well beyond the extent
of any single track.

"Simplify to N points" binary-searches the tolerance that lands under the
budget. Fidelity is checked by the harness: reducing a 163 km planned route
from **3598 to 494 points** — an 86% reduction — changes total distance by
**0.35 km, or 0.2%**. The harness asserts this stays within 2% for tracks up to
a day-sized recording; reduce a 15 000-point track that far and the drift is
geometry, not a bug.

## What the harness checks

`npm run verify` parses every file in `gpx/` and asserts:

- Every file yields at least one track, with elevation and coordinate arrays in
  step
- Round-tripping through the writer preserves point count, timestamps, elevation
  values, waypoints and the track name
- Output declares the GPX 1.1 namespace and escapes `&`, `<`, `>` and `"`
- `<rte>`-only files load; so do files with `lon` before `lat`, single-quoted
  attributes, and self-closing `<trkpt/>`
- A missing `<ele>` yields `null` rather than dropping the point
- Trim, reverse, simplify and stage-splitting preserve the invariants above
- The noise floor rejects jitter and preserves genuine climbs

97 checks at the time of writing, all passing, in about 25 seconds.
